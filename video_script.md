**[0:00-1:00 | Notebook title and summary]**
Hi, I am Muntaser Syed. This is my submission for the quanTA geometry-aware quantum cloud challenge. The task is to prepare a verified Bell pair, the Phi-plus state, at two designated ports on each of three processor models, and to compare an ideal baseline, a noisy baseline, and one error-detection strategy across all three. My headline result is simple to state: under the common noise model, fidelity is decided by how many two-qubit gates the routing forces on you, not by how many qubits the processor has. And the protection strategy I tested, a single parity check, only pays for itself when the check itself needs no routing. I will show the numbers behind both claims.

**[1:00-2:30 | Section 2, coupling graph figure]**
Here are the three models. The 2016-inspired reference has 5 qubits, with ports 0 and 4, two hops apart. The contemporary IBM-style model has 9 qubits, ports 0 and 7, four hops apart. The hyperbolic-inspired model has 13 qubits, ports 0 and 12, three hops apart. Keep those hop counts in mind: 2, 4, and 3. The red rings are the ports. The gold diamonds are the parity-check ancillas, and I will explain in a few minutes how they were chosen, because the choice was made by a fixed rule before any fidelity was computed.

**[2:30-4:00 | Sections 3 and 4, compilation and baselines]**
The method follows the starter notebook exactly. I place the Bell circuit, H on the first qubit and a CX to the second, on the ports, route it with Qiskit's basic router, then apply the routing SWAPs in reverse so the pair ends up back on the designated ports, with each SWAP expanded into three CX gates. That means the CX count is one plus six times the number of outbound SWAPs: 7 CX for the 2016 model, 19 for the contemporary model, and 13 for the hyperbolic model. The noise model is the common one from the prompt: a two-qubit depolarizing channel at 0.02 after every CX, including SWAP CXs, with ideal H gates. Fidelity is computed from the three Pauli correlations, XX, YY, and ZZ, on the reduced state of the ports, exactly as the prompt specifies. In the ideal case every model gives fidelity exactly 1, which verifies the delivery. That assertion is in the notebook.

**[4:00-5:30 | Section 4 output and Section 6 table, Cases 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8]**
Under noise, the ordering is 0.915 for the 2016 model, 0.852 for the hyperbolic model, and 0.795 for the contemporary model. That is exactly the CX-count ordering, and it is not the size ordering: the 13-qubit hyperbolic model beats the 9-qubit contemporary model because its ports are one hop closer, which removes six noisy CX gates. One more observation: in every model the XX and YY correlations decay faster than ZZ, for example 0.68 against 0.82 on the contemporary model. So the loss is mostly lost phase coherence. A Z-basis-only check would have overstated every result.

**[5:30-8:00 | Section 5, protection design and ancilla scan]**
Now the protection strategy, and there is exactly one: a ZZ parity check. After the pair is delivered to the ports, one ancilla starting in zero receives a CX from each port. For Phi-plus the port bits agree, so the ancilla should stay at zero. A single bit-flip error on a port flips it to one, and we discard those shots. The check can only see odd-weight bit flips that happen before or during the check. It is blind to phase errors, and blind to anything that happens afterwards, including noise on its own return SWAPs. Whether it helps is therefore an empirical question. The ancilla was not cherry-picked. I compiled the check segment for every candidate qubit and selected by a fixed rule: fewest outbound SWAPs in the compiled check, then shortest worst-case distance to the ports, then lowest index. That gives ancilla 3 on the 2016 model, which is adjacent to both ports and costs zero SWAPs and two CX. Ancilla 2 on the contemporary model, at two outbound SWAPs and 14 CX. And ancilla 1 on the hyperbolic model, at one outbound SWAP and 8 CX.

**[8:00-10:00 | Section 6 table and charts, Cases 3, 6, 9]**
Here is the payoff, or lack of it. On the 2016 model the check works: conditional fidelity rises from 0.915 to 0.941, and 91.7 percent of shots survive. On the contemporary model it does not: conditional fidelity is 0.769, below the 0.795 baseline, even after discarding 15.2 percent of shots. On the hyperbolic model it is close to break-even: 0.847 against 0.852, discarding 10.8 percent. The pattern tracks the check cost exactly: two extra CX gains, eight extra CX roughly breaks even, fourteen extra CX clearly loses. Postselection does detect real errors; if you ignore the ancilla, the protected circuits are worse, 0.888, 0.661, and 0.762, and conditioning recovers much of that. But the detected error class is only part of depolarizing noise, and the check segment injects fresh noise of every class, some of it after the parity is copied, where nothing can detect it.

**[10:00-11:30 | Section 7, shot cross-check]**
A quick word on trust in these numbers. The headline values are exact density-matrix results for the stated noise model, so they have no sampling error. In Section 7 I repeated all nine cases the way the example notebook does, measuring in the three bases with 8192 shots each and keeping only ancilla-zero shots for the protected cases. Every shot estimate agrees with the exact value within about two standard errors, with uncertainties of roughly plus or minus 0.002 to 0.004. I also reran the comparison at noise strengths 0.01 and 0.05 in the appendix: the sign of the protection effect never changes, only its size.

**[11:30-13:00 | Sections 8 and 9, limitations and recommendation]**
Limitations, briefly. The noise model is one synthetic channel: no readout error, no relaxation, no crosstalk, ideal single-qubit gates. Compilation uses the starter notebook's basic router at optimization level zero for every case, on purpose, so all models share one policy; a stronger router would lower every CX count. And these are small finite graphs, so the hyperbolic result is a statement about these two ports on this graph, not a universal claim about hyperbolic architectures. My one recommendation follows directly from the data: build a dedicated parity-check ancilla into the port geometry, coupled directly to both ports of a designated pair, so the check costs two CX and zero SWAPs. That geometry already exists by accident in the 2016 model, and it is the only case where protection gained anything. Error detection at a port is a connectivity feature, not just a circuit feature. The notebook is fully reproducible: it asserts that every CX lies on the coupling graph and that every ideal fidelity is exactly one. Thank you.
